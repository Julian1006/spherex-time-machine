    What am I looking at?

    These are real images

    Every pixel comes from a Level‑2 SPHEREx image in NASA/IPAC's public archive. Each file is 71 MB, so instead of downloading them whole I only read the small piece around each target from the nasa-irsa-spherex bucket on AWS. Those pieces come bundled with the page, which is why it loads fast. Brightness is in MJy/sr, straight from the pipeline.

    SPHEREx launched in March 2025 and covers the whole sky every six months. It has no filter wheel. Its filter changes color across the detector, so the wavelength you get depends on where a star lands. That's why every visit shows a different wavelength.

    Lining the frames up

    The telescope is turned a different way every time it comes back, so the raw images don't match. I reprojected all of them onto the same grid (north up, east left, 6.15″ per pixel) using the TAN‑SIP solution stored in each file.

    So if something jumps between two frames, it really moved in the sky. It's not the camera.

    To check it, I took stars in the deep field that were seen at the same wavelength months apart. Their brightness matches to about 1%, and that only happens if the alignment, the wavelengths and the photometry are all right.

    Things to try

    1. Hit play and watch. Normal stars don't move.
    2. Pin a frame, then switch to Difference. If it's flat grey, nothing changed. A black dot next to a white one means something moved.
    3. Zoom in with + or a pinch. A nearby star shifts only a pixel or two in a year, and from far away you'd miss it.
    4. Add images together to pull faint things out of the grain. Do it season by season and you can blink one season against another.
    5. Tick Gaia or WISE to see which dots are stars already in the catalogues, and the flagged pixels to see which specks NASA's own software distrusts.
    6. Click a star to see its brightness at each wavelength, or date by date. Asteroids move within minutes; variable stars take months to brighten and fade.

    Heads up: the spectra are quick estimates for exploring. They're not calibrated measurements.

    What the frame info means

    The box next to the image describes the exposure you're looking at. Here's what each line means.

    LineWhat it means
    ObservedThe calendar date this exposure was taken.
    MJDThe same instant as a running count of days, so the gap between two visits is just a subtraction. Day zero was 17 November 1858.
    WavelengthThe exact infrared colour this frame recorded. It is different on almost every visit, because SPHEREx has no filter wheel — what a pixel catches depends on where the source lands on the detector.
    BandwidthHow wide a slice of colour the frame covers. R is the wavelength divided by that width, so a higher R means a finer slice.
    DetectorWhich of the six detector arrays caught the target, and the stretch of infrared that array covers.
    ExposureSPHEREx's own name for this exposure: the year, the week of the survey, which segment of it, and the number within that segment.
    ReleaseThe public data release it belongs to. deep marks a pointing at one of the two deep fields, which are revisited far more often than the rest of the sky.
    Source fileA link to the original 71 MB file in NASA's archive. I only downloaded a small square of it. Click it, it's the fun one.
    DistanceHow far the body was from Earth at that moment, in astronomical units. Shown only for the moving targets.
    NotesWarnings the pipeline attached to this exposure: a failed quality check, rough astrometry, light from an aurora or the glow of the upper atmosphere. Shown only for images read live.
    Blank pixelsPixels with no brightness at all in this frame, usually where a bright star overwhelmed the detector or the pixel is dead. Those are the dark specks. I left them as they are.
    Flagged pixelsShown once you tick Pixels NASA flagged as doubtful: how many pixels of this frame the pipeline warned about, and why. A cosmic-ray hit, a dead pixel, a star too bright for the detector, the ghost of one, or the streak of a satellite.
    Added upShown when images are added together: how many exposures went into the picture, the dates and wavelengths they span, and how much the grain dropped.

    About the data

    Nothing here is simulated or edited. Every frame is a piece of one real SPHEREx exposure, and each one links to its original file, so you can check it yourself.

    Brightness values are the ones the pipeline measured, in MJy/sr. Nothing is rescaled or touched up, and images are only added together when you ask for it. Data: NASA/JPL‑Caltech/IPAC.

    SPHEREx Time Machine Real images from NASA's SPHEREx telescope, read from the public archive at NASA/IPAC IRSA. Star positions from Gaia and WISE through CDS VizieR, names from SIMBAD, known asteroids from IMCCE SkyBoT.
